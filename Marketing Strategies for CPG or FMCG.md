# Marketing Strategies for CPG/FMCG

## Reference and context

These notes are based on a course I took on marketing strategies for CPG/FMCG clients. The framework below organizes the course notes into four marketing strategies and the execution variables used to bring each strategy to life.

## 1. What is a marketing strategy?

A marketing strategy is a plan of action to promote and sell a product or service. In CPG/FMCG, a strategy can help a brand grow its business, defend or increase market share, attract a particular consumer segment, or improve value creation within an existing category.

The four strategies covered here are:

1. Premiumization
2. Me-too
3. Volume Seeker
4. Niche

These are strategic choices, not rigid boxes. A brand can combine elements of more than one strategy, but it should be clear about its primary objective and target consumer.

## 2. Premiumization

### Definition

Premiumization means selling a product at a higher price than the category or segment average. The higher price should be supported by a consumer-relevant reason to pay more, such as superior product quality, differentiated features, better ingredients, distinctive packaging, stronger brand positioning, or a more elevated consumption experience.

### Typical characteristics

- Higher average selling price (ASP) than the category or segment average.
- Differentiated product design, quality, ingredients, features, or experience.
- Premium brand positioning and communication.
- Often requires above-average advertising or consumer education.
- Can support higher margins, but usually needs upfront investment and patience because returns may take time.
- Difficult to sustain without a meaningful point of difference.

### Illustrative Indian CPG example

Doritos and other premium-positioned corn-based snacks can illustrate premiumization when they command a price premium versus more conventional snack alternatives through distinctive flavours, product format, branding, packaging, and an elevated snacking proposition. The example is illustrative: whether a product is premium should be assessed against its specific category, pack size, channel, and price benchmarks.

### When it makes sense

Premiumization is useful when consumers recognize and value the difference, the category has room for a higher-priced proposition, and the brand can support that proposition through product, communication, availability, and retail execution.

### Risks

A higher price alone does not create a premium brand. If consumers do not perceive a meaningful difference, the product may lose value share or struggle to justify its price. Premiumization can also require greater investment and may take longer to scale.

## 3. Me-too strategy

### Definition

A me-too strategy follows an established product or brand proposition in a category. The offer may be similar to the category leader or to an existing successful product, rather than introducing a fundamentally new proposition. It competes by offering a familiar alternative, often with a price, value, or availability advantage.

### Private-label example: BigBasket

BigBasket's private-label portfolio can be used to explain how retailers may serve different price-value tiers. BigBasket Royal can be discussed as a more premium-oriented private-label proposition, while BigBasket's regular/private-label offerings can serve more mainstream needs. The exact product range and positioning can vary by category, so compare products within the same category and pack size.

This example also shows an important distinction: a retailer can use a tiered private-label architecture rather than having one single private-label proposition. A premium tier and a regular tier can target different consumer expectations and willingness to pay.

### Typical characteristics

- The core proposition is similar to an existing product or established category offer.
- The strategy may focus on an attractive price, reliable quality, or convenient availability.
- It may use established category cues rather than educating consumers about a completely new concept.
- Retailer private labels can use me-too products to provide a lower-priced alternative to national brands or to create multiple price tiers.

### Risks

If the offer is too similar and has no clear price, quality, availability, or trust advantage, it can become easy to substitute. A me-too strategy should not be confused with copying protected designs, trademarks, or other intellectual property.

## 4. Volume Seeker strategy

### Definition

A volume seeker strategy aims to sell a large number of units and grow penetration or market share. The product does not necessarily need to be the cheapest in the category, but the proposition is designed to appeal to a broad consumer base and encourage repeat purchases.

### Typical characteristics

- Broad target audience and relatively wide distribution.
- Accessible price points, pack-price architecture, or promotional offers.
- Strong availability and visibility across relevant channels.
- Communication designed to reach a broad audience.
- Focus on units sold, penetration, market share, and repeat purchase.
- The strategy may prioritize scale over a very high price premium or highly specialized differentiation.

### Illustrative Indian CPG examples

Mainstream products such as Lay's potato chips, Kurkure, or mass-market carbonated soft drinks can illustrate volume-oriented execution when brands use broad distribution, familiar flavours, multiple pack sizes, and wide-reach communication to drive frequent purchases. These brands can also use premium or niche initiatives in specific sub-ranges; the classification depends on the product and the objective being assessed.

### Risks

High volume does not automatically mean high profit. Price discounts, trade spending, and distribution costs can reduce margins. Volume growth should therefore be evaluated alongside net revenue, contribution, and profitability.

## 5. Niche strategy

### Definition

A niche strategy focuses on a specific consumer group, need state, usage occasion, or underserved segment. Rather than targeting the whole category, the brand aims to be particularly relevant to a narrower audience.

### Typical characteristics

- A clearly defined audience or consumer need.
- A focused proposition that feels distinctive or especially relevant to that audience.
- Potentially higher price or stronger margins when the target group values the offer.
- More selective distribution and communication than a mass-market strategy.
- Success depends on understanding the target segment deeply, not simply making the product expensive.

### Example: Noise by Swiggy

The “Noise” example mentioned in the notes should be verified for the exact product or service meant before publishing. If it is a Swiggy offering designed for a specific audience or consumption occasion, it can illustrate niche positioning when it solves a distinct need for that segment. It may also have premiumization elements if the offer supports a higher price through added value. Niche and premiumization are not mutually exclusive: niche describes whom the offer serves, while premiumization describes its price-value positioning.

### Risks

A niche may be too small to support the desired scale, and demand can be concentrated in a narrow audience. The brand needs to balance segment relevance, willingness to pay, acquisition cost, and the size of the addressable market.

## 6. The 13 execution variables

Execution variables are the practical levers a brand uses to translate its strategy into market outcomes. The handwritten notes organize the levers around the marketing mix: Product, Price, Promotion, and Place.

The notes clearly identify these examples and labels: product quality, product features, product requirements, everyday/regular price, promotional price, advertising, media investment, consumer promotions, distribution, shelf placement, and retail margin. The handwritten page appears to include additional price/promotion/place distinctions; the list below consolidates the 13 levers into a usable working framework. Check the labels against the original course material before treating the wording as a verbatim course list.

### Product

1. **Product quality** — How well the product performs against consumer expectations and category standards.
2. **Product features** — Functional or sensory attributes that differentiate the offer, such as flavour, format, ingredients, or convenience.
3. **Product requirements** — The product proposition and specifications required by the target consumer and category, including relevant pack or usage needs.

### Price

4. **Everyday/regular price** — The standard shelf price that establishes the product's everyday value position.
5. **Promotional price** — A temporary or offer-led price used to stimulate trial, accelerate purchase, or respond to competition.
6. **Price premium / price positioning** — The price difference versus a relevant category, segment, or competitor benchmark; it indicates whether the offer is positioned as value, mainstream, or premium.

### Promotion

7. **Advertising** — The message and communication used to build awareness, explain benefits, and shape brand perceptions.
8. **Media investment** — The amount and allocation of spend across media and channels to reach the intended audience.
9. **Consumer promotions** — Short-term mechanisms such as discounts, offers, bundles, or sampling that encourage trial or purchase.

### Place and retail execution

10. **Distribution** — The breadth and quality of availability across relevant stores, channels, and geographies.
11. **Shelf placement** — The product's visibility and location in-store, including whether it is easy for shoppers to find.
12. **Retailer/trade margin** — The economics offered to retailers and channel partners, which can influence willingness to stock, recommend, and support the product.
13. **In-store visibility / merchandising** — Displays, facings, signage, and other point-of-sale execution that help the product stand out and convert shopper attention into purchase.

**Note on fidelity:** The handwritten notes are clear on the 4Ps and most of the listed examples, but not every one of the 13 labels is fully legible. “Price premium/price positioning” and “in-store visibility/merchandising” are included to complete a practical 13-variable framework. If the course used different exact labels, replace these two with the original terms.

## 7. How execution variables differ across the four strategies

The same execution variable can be used in all four strategies, but the role it plays changes with the strategy.

| Execution area | Premiumization | Me-too | Volume Seeker | Niche |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Product quality | Clearly superior or perceptibly elevated | Meets category expectations reliably | Consistent quality at scale | Tailored to the target group's needs |
| Product features | Distinctive features justify a premium | Familiar features similar to established offers | Easy-to-understand benefits for a broad audience | Specialized features solve a specific need |
| Product requirements | Elevated experience and premium cues | Meets expected category specifications | Broad appeal and convenient formats | Designed around a specific use case or segment |
| Everyday/regular price | Above relevant category benchmark | Competitive with comparable alternatives | Accessible price points to support repeat purchase | Set according to niche value and willingness to pay |
| Promotional price | Used carefully to protect premium perception | Can encourage switching or trial | Often used to stimulate units and penetration | Targeted offers can recruit the intended segment |
| Price premium/positioning | Premium must be supported by perceived value | Usually parity or a clear value advantage | May prioritize affordability and scale | Can be premium, mainstream, or value-led |
| Advertising | Explains differentiation and reinforces desirability | Builds familiarity and communicates the alternative | Broad-reach communication supports awareness and frequency | Focused communication reaches the defined audience |
| Media investment | Invests to build premium meaning and educate consumers | Often efficient and focused on clear points of difference | Broad reach and efficient cost per contact | Concentrated investment in relevant audiences/channels |
| Consumer promotions | Selective, to avoid weakening premium cues | Supports trial and value comparison | Frequently used to accelerate purchase volume | Designed for the specific audience or occasion |
| Distribution | Selective or broad, depending on the premium proposition | Strong availability where category buyers shop | Wide distribution is often critical | Selective distribution where the niche audience shops |
| Shelf placement | Premium block, visibility, and quality cues | Easy comparison with category alternatives | High availability and prominent placement | Placement near relevant shoppers or adjacent solutions |
| Retailer/trade margin | Must support premium economics and retailer motivation | Competitive economics can help secure listings | Economics must work at scale despite high volumes | Can reflect specialist needs, but must remain viable |
| In-store visibility/merchandising | Reinforces quality and distinctiveness | Helps shoppers notice and compare the offer | Drives attention and quick conversion | Makes the specialized benefit easy to recognize |

## 8. A practical way to choose the strategy

Before choosing a strategy, answer these questions:

- **Consumer:** Who are we trying to serve, and what need are we solving?
- **Category:** Is the opportunity to charge more, match an established offer, sell more units, or serve an underserved segment?
- **Value proposition:** What makes the product worth choosing?
- **Economics:** What do price, net revenue, margin, and cost-to-serve look like?
- **Execution:** Can the product, price, promotion, and place choices consistently deliver the intended positioning?
- **Measurement:** Which metrics will indicate success: average selling price, value share, unit share, penetration, repeat purchase, distribution, or contribution?

A useful rule of thumb is that **premiumization is about earning a higher price; me-too is about offering a familiar alternative; volume seeking is about scaling unit sales and reach; and niche is about serving a specific audience or need especially well.** A brand may combine these, but the primary strategic intent should guide execution.
